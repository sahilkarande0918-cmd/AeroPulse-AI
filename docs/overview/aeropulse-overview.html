AeroPulse AI

Predict early. Maintain smarter. Fly ready.

AeroPulse AI is a web application that helps aircraft maintenance teams answer three questions: which engine needs attention, can we trust the computer's warning, and what is the best plan with the technicians and spare parts we actually have? It is an advisory tool — it suggests, people decide.

The problem, in plain words

Planes wait on the ground for the wrong reasons

Military air fleets lose availability because maintenance is mostly reactive — things are fixed after they break — and the information needed to act early lives in separate places: engine sensor data, paper or digital technical logs, spare-part stores, and technician rosters.

When these don't talk to each other, faults are spotted late, aircraft wait for parts nobody ordered, and work is scheduled without knowing which job matters most.

What we built

One platform, five jobs

1 · CollectImports sensor data, maintenance records, spare parts and work orders; rejects bad or conflicting rows.
2 · PredictA machine-learning model estimates each engine's remaining life, with a confidence range.
3 · Check trustTrustTwin tests the input data and refuses to give a number when it can't be trusted.
4 · PlanAn optimiser builds a work schedule that respects skills, shifts, parts and task order.
5 · ApproveAn officer approves or rejects with a reason; every action is recorded.
How it works, end to end

Maintenance officer

Reviews evidence; approves or rejects with a reason.

Technician

Sees assigned jobs; marks them started or done.

Analyst

Explores data and runs what-ifs; cannot approve.

Administrator

Manages users, roles and thresholds.

Important: AeroPulse never decides whether an aircraft may fly. It is a prototype trained on public, simulated engine data, and every aircraft, part and person inside it is fictional.

The data and the machine-learning model

Teaching a computer how engines wear out

Where the data comes from

We use NASA's public C-MAPSS dataset (“Turbofan Engine Degradation Simulation”). NASA simulated jet engines running flight after flight until they wore out, recording 21 sensors — temperatures, pressures, fan and core speeds — once per flight cycle. We use the subset FD001: 100 engines to learn from, 100 separate engines to test on, one flight condition and one kind of wear (the high-pressure compressor).

Downloaded from NASA's official archive and fingerprint-checked (SHA-256) so anyone can reproduce it. It is simulated commercial-engine data — useful for building and testing the method, not proof about military aircraft.

What the model predicts: Remaining Useful Life

How the model learns — in four steps

Step 1 · Clean

Drop 9 sensors that never change, keep 12 useful ones.

Step 2 · Describe

For each sensor: recent average, how much it wobbles, and its trend — using only past flights, never the future.

Step 3 · Split fairly

Whole engines go into separate groups, so the model is always tested on engines it has never seen.

Step 4 · Compare

Try a simple average, a straight-line model and LightGBM; keep whichever is most accurate on unseen engines.

The chosen model: LightGBM

LightGBM builds hundreds of small decision trees. Each tree asks simple questions (“is the compressor temperature trend above X?”) and each new tree fixes the mistakes of the ones before it. It is fast, runs on an ordinary laptop CPU, and — importantly — its reasoning can be explained exactly.

*RMSE (root-mean-square error) and MAE (mean absolute error). When an engine has ≤ 50 cycles left — the moment that matters — the error drops to 8.2 cycles.

Not just a number — a range

A single guess like “13 cycles left” hides uncertainty. AeroPulse says “between 0 and 26 cycles, with 90% confidence”, using a method called conformal prediction. We checked it on engines the model never saw: the true answer fell inside the range 89–90% of the time — the confidence is measured, not assumed.

Why did it say that? — SHAP

Every prediction lists which sensor trends pulled the estimate down (red) or up (green), in cycles.

We also benchmarked NASA's three harder subsets (several flight conditions, two kinds of wear). LightGBM still beat the simpler models everywhere (errors 17–27 cycles), but the app uses the FD001 model, where results are strongest.

What makes AeroPulse different

TrustTwin: “Can we trust this — and what is the safest plan?”

Most predictive-maintenance projects stop at a prediction. Real maintenance is messier: sensors fail, data arrives late, spare parts run out and technicians go on leave. AeroPulse TrustTwin is the layer that handles that reality before anyone acts.

The what-if simulator, as a story

Engine AP-ENG-01 is wearing out

The model predicts about 13 cycles left (range 0–26). All checks pass → VALID. Recommendation: plan a module replacement.

The spare part is out of stock

The optimiser re-plans with the real stock and delivery date. If the part can't arrive in time, it says so — it never hides the conflict.

Two sensors stop reporting

TrustTwin downgrades to DEGRADED and suggests an inspection first. The officer approves with a reason; it's logged.

The planner: a puzzle solver for maintenance

We use Google OR-Tools CP-SAT, a constraint solver. We give it rules that can never be broken — the right skill, working shifts and leave, parts in stock or arriving, tasks in the correct order, one job per aircraft at a time — and a goal: finish the most urgent work first with the fewest late jobs. A separate checker re-verifies every plan, and a simple rule-based scheduler is kept for comparison. Jobs that can't fit are listed with the exact reason.

Honest result from our fleet simulation (reactive vs predictive maintenance, same events): predictive planning removed all 20 unexpected engine failures, but overall availability barely moved (92.0% → 92.2%) because early replacements use up spare parts. We report this exactly as computed — it is a simulation, not a promise about real fleets.
